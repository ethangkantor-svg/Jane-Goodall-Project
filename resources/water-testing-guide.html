Water-Quality Data Collection Guide

A printable one-pager from the Westchester Youth Waterway Initiative, a Roots & Shoots (Jane Goodall Institute) project.

Why we test the water

Litter is the problem you can see. Water quality tells you about the problems you can't. Alongside our cleanups, we take basic readings at the same handful of spots along the pathway so we can track how the river is doing over time, not just how it looks on any one day.

What we measure

MeasurementWhat it tells usHow we take it
pHHow acidic or basic the water is. Healthy freshwater is usually close to neutral.Test strip or digital pH pen dipped in a fresh sample
TemperatureAffects oxygen levels and what species can live there. Big swings can signal a problem upstream.Waterproof thermometer, held below the surface for 30 seconds
TurbidityHow cloudy the water is, a sign of runoff, erosion, or disturbed sediment.Turbidity tube or comparison against a clarity chart
Dissolved oxygenHow much oxygen is available for fish and other aquatic life.Dissolved oxygen test kit (color-change reagent)

How we record it

  • Every reading gets logged on a data sheet with the date, site name, time, and volunteer initials.
  • We test at the same fixed points each time, so results are actually comparable month to month.
  • Anything that looks unusual, like an odd smell, color, or reading far outside the normal range, gets flagged and double-checked.

Why consistency matters

A single reading doesn't tell you much on its own. Water conditions change with weather, season, and time of day. What actually matters is testing the same spots, the same way, on a regular schedule, so patterns show up over time. That consistency is what turns a few numbers on a clipboard into real environmental data.

Want to help collect data? This is one of the roles we're always looking for volunteers for. No science background required, just attention to detail. Reach out through our Contact page.
